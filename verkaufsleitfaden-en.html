Sales Guide · Solar Consultants

Selling solar convincingly.
From first contact to close.

A practical guide for consultative sales conversations with solar customers: attitude, opening the conversation, needs analysis, argumentation and closing.

01 Attitude and goal

Selling doesn't begin in the conversation — it begins in your attitude before it. Four areas need constant attention, and you sit at the centre of all of them:

  • People — your relationship with customers, colleagues, partners
  • Time and organisation — preparation, follow-up, structure in your day-to-day work
  • Technical knowledge — the technical and commercial foundation no argument can do without
Success = goal achieved. No goal, no success. Before you walk into a consultation or a price negotiation, set yourself a SMART goal:
  • Specific
  • Measurable
  • Ambitious
  • Realistic
  • Time-bound
  • and framed positively

Two attitudes the hands-on workshop singled out as central:

The courage to take risks. Selling means exposing yourself to contact — the highs (satisfaction) and the lows (setbacks) alike. Whoever avoids contact to dodge the risk of a "no" also gives up the chance of a "yes".
Turn customers into fans. The real goal of a good conversation isn't just the individual sale — it's the active, voluntary referral that follows. Whoever becomes a fan keeps selling for you.

02 Your personal impact

One point stood out in the workshop: your personal impact is decisive — more so than technical knowledge on its own. The SCIL model breaks it down:

ImpactMeans
SensusEmotional impact — what does your presence trigger in the customer?
CorpusPhysical impact — posture, gestures, facial expression, eye contact
IntellectusIntellectual impact — expertise, clarity of your arguments
LinguaVerbal impact — word choice, tone, phrasing

Building rapport

Before content can convince anyone, the connection with the person across from you has to be right. Pay conscious attention to:

Posture Gestures Facial expression Eye contact Voice Feeling

And in the "red zone" — when things get tense (price negotiation, objections, conflict) — hold on to your own tone, calm and composure, and let your expertise show. According to the workshop, that's exactly what separates a good salesperson from a mediocre one.

"I bow to no one, and no one has to bow to me. We are all equals." — quoted in the sales training

A negotiation isn't about beating the other side, and it isn't about losing to them either. It's about having a good conversation that ends with both sides able to say: "That was worth it!"

Good salespeople can handle tension. Between what the customer wants to pay and what you've calculated, there's almost always tension. That's normal — it's part of the process. Sit with it instead of caving in too soon or pretending it isn't there. More on this under Pricing.

03 The conversation roadmap

Every sales conversation moves through four phases from first contact to yes-or-no — like a funnel narrowing toward a decision:

1. Opening — earn attention, "different from everyone else"see chapter 4
2. Discovery — open questions, understand the needsee chapters 5–6
3. Argumentation — Problem, Solution, Benefit (PSB)see chapters 7–8
4. Closing — read buying signals, guide to a yessee chapter 10

The need you're addressing is always aligned strategically with the person — not just with the product.

04 Opening the conversation

The goal of the opening: earn attention, win the customer's engagement, build the connection. Good opening lines come from four areas:

AreaExamples
General situationPrice trends, the market and regulatory situation, subsidy programmes
The customer's situationBuilding projects, renovations, structural changes, their current electricity bill
Your own situationReference projects, awards, new services, cost comparisons
Your own offerNew modules/batteries, product additions, current terms
Remember: Open the conversation differently from everyone else. Whoever starts with the same line as every other salesperson has already given away the first chance to be noticed.

05 Needs analysis

Open vs. closed questions

TypeHow to spot itWhen to use itExamples
Open question starts with a question word (what, how, where, which …) mostly at the start of a conversation — forces a detailed answer "What exactly are you looking for?" · "What do you expect from your system?"
Closed question starts with a verb, answer is yes/no to close out a phase of the conversation or confirm something "If I understand correctly, that matters more to you?" · "May I note that down?"

Building on the positive — the strawberry method

Look closely, pick up every positive signal, and keep digging in the same spot — like picking strawberries in the forest. The customer says: "I think your offer is basically good." You pick it up: "You think the offer is basically good — what do you like about it specifically?"

Letting the negative lie

  • Let it pass: customer says "The product would be useful, but I don't like the colour" → you respond only to "useful".
  • Agree, then dig back into the positive: "You're right — and does the performance otherwise match what you're looking for?"
Don't argue it away. A rust spot is a rust spot. Hours of debate only cement the negative more firmly in the customer's memory.

Keep searching if nothing turns up

No luck in one spot → change the subject, ask in the opposite direction: "I pictured this differently." → "How did you picture it?" If the customer keeps circling back to the same negative point, switch to a question that's about them personally — that's what people are most interested in, after all.

Listen and let them finish

People tend to mention the minor stuff first and get to what really matters later. Let the customer finish speaking, then wait another second or two before you respond — that's often exactly when the crucial detail comes out.

Justify your questions

An unexplained question can feel intrusive. "Which months bring in your highest income?" sounds nosy. "I'm only asking because I'd like to work out payment terms that suit you" makes the very same question welcome.

06 Understanding customer motives

What does the customer actually care about? Six motive categories, with typical solar examples from practice:

MotiveExamples in solar sales
Money, profitMake a sound investment, cut heating costs, get a good deal
SecurityBeing able to rely on it, raising the quality of the house, supply security
ConvenienceEverything from one provider, not having to worry about anything, a smooth installation
Curiosity, knowledgeNew technology on the house, using sustainable energy, trying something new
Social connectionA pleasant working relationship, enjoying the build, a partner for the long run
Status, prestigeExclusivity, a special offer, preferential treatment
On weighting these. Drawing on S. Reiss's model of human motives, the workshop discussed a rough order of how strongly these motives typically drive buying decisions: status/prestige, social connection, curiosity/knowledge, convenience, security, money/profit (descending). Treat this as a rough rule of thumb from the training, not a measured statistic for solar sales — useful as food for thought, not as hard data: price is rarely the strongest buying motive, even though it's what customers talk about most.

07 Arguing convincingly: the PSB model

Strong arguments always follow the same bridge:

P — Problem — name the underlying need so the customer becomes aware of it: "What matters to you here is that …" / "As you told me …" / "You'd surely also like …"What's really at stake for them?
S — Solution — name the matching detail: a fact, a product, a service, an offer detail, a strength of the companyWhat solves it?
B — Benefit — build the bridge to what's in it for the customerWhat do they get out of it?

Phrases for building the benefit bridge

Detail + bridge, e.g. "This solar solution …":

means for you … gets you … increases your … lowers your … gives you … you'll end up with … brings you … grows your … shows you … provides you … saves you … guarantees you …

Important: the argument has to connect back to the interests you uncovered earlier (chapters 5–6) — not to a generic list of product features.

08 Our strongest arguments

The workshop's discussion suggests one plausible ranking of the strongest sales arguments for solar products:

RankArgument
1Technical expertise
2Worry-free package (everything from one provider)
3Local presence
4Years of experience
5High-quality products

Judged too generic to carry an argument on their own: "turnkey system", "components from X", "regional" — without a concrete benefit attached, buzzwords like these don't tell the customer much.

Not a fixed template. This order shifts by region and audience: commercial customers often weigh return on investment more heavily, rural private customers tend to value local presence more, and in more urban or international markets, brand and certifications can matter more. Treat this list as a starting point for your own audience, not a rigid rule.
Takeaway: Technical expertise beats plain product or provenance arguments ("turnkey", "X years of experience"). Customers buy less of the hardware and more the confidence that someone competent is accountable for the whole process.

09 Price negotiation: handling tension

Between what the customer is willing to pay and what you've calculated, tension builds up. That tension is normal — it isn't a sign the conversation has failed.

Customer (C) ── tension ── Salesperson (S)

Illustrative example: if a customer offers CHF 30,000 for a project calculated at CHF 35,000, don't bridge the gap by caving in right away — bridge it with argumentation (chapters 7–8): what exactly justifies the difference — expertise, a worry-free package, warranty terms? The figures here are purely illustrative; in a real conversation, your actual calculation stays confidential.

Stay in the "red zone" deliberately calm, composed, and in your own tone (chapter 2) — caving in out of insecurity undermines your own argument.

10 Closing techniques

Reading buying signals

According to the training, four out of five salespeople don't consciously watch for buying signals. Questions, remarks and body language show how ready to buy a customer is:

SignalMeaningExamples
Redwarning light, resistant"Why would I agree to these terms?" · arms tightly crossed
Blueneutral, neither for nor against"Hmm, I don't know anything about that" · head resting on hand
Greengreen light, positively inclined"That's an amount we could invest" · animated expression, leaning forward

Direct vs. indirect close

Direct closeIndirect close
TechniqueA closed question — "May I confirm the order on that basis?"Collecting small "yeses" — several small confirmations before the actual close
RuleWorks almost every time, once there are enough green signalsUse at the end of every conversation — even when no order results (yet)
When it's mandatoryAfter a price negotiation, in the follow-up callAlways, as the way you end the conversation
The last impression sticks. Even a conversation without a close deserves a clean, friendly ending — it helps decide whether the customer comes back later or refers you on.

11 Practice checklist

  1. Before the conversation: set a SMART goal, prepare an opening from one of the 4 areas (chapter 4), research the customer's situation.
  2. Opening: different from everyone else, show genuine friendliness, leave the customer room to open the conversation.
  3. Discovery: ask open questions, build on the positive, let the negative lie, let them finish speaking.
  4. Argumentation: name the need (Problem), name the matching detail (Solution), build the bridge to the benefit — matched to the motive you uncovered (chapter 6).
  5. Under price pressure: stay calm, argue with expertise rather than with a discount.
  6. Closing: watch for buying signals (traffic light), close directly on green, otherwise close indirectly with a clean, collected "yes".
  7. After the conversation: schedule the follow-up, keep every promise — that's what referrals are built on.
